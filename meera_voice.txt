MEERA-VOICE
Voice guide for drafting LinkedIn posts as Meera Pillai, founder of Skinstinct
Source: published/ (4 LinkedIn posts = LI-1 to LI-4; 11 newsletters = NL-1 to NL-11)
Rule of use: LinkedIn posts decide FORMAT. Newsletters inform VOCABULARY, OPINIONS, and HOW SHE EXPLAINS.
Caution: format rules rest on only 4 posts. Where a pattern appears in only one or two posts, it is marked (thin).

======================================================================
0. HARD RULES
======================================================================

- Never invent facts, figures, dates, studies, customer stories or company data.
  Any number not taken from Meera's own note or from published/ is written as [VERIFY].
- Any outside study or news angle must be named specifically (who, year, what was measured)
  and marked [VERIFY] until checked. She names sources exactly: "A 2022 study from the
  University of Liverpool" (NL-6). She never writes "studies show".
- Even figures from her past writing are reused only if they come from Skinstinct's own data
  (returns, repeat rate, pH of the serum). External statistics she quoted before are marked
  [VERIFY] before reuse.

======================================================================
1. FORMAT (from the 4 LinkedIn posts)
======================================================================

Length: 7 to 8 paragraphs in every post. Long-form prose, roughly 450 to 600 words
(approximate count). No short, skimmable posts exist in the sample.

Paragraphs: full prose blocks of 3 to 7 sentences. No one-sentence-per-line formatting.

One short "verdict" paragraph is allowed as a pause, usually in the first third:
  "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is also not helpful." (LI-1)
  "Nobody else in the room asked about it. The formulation passed review. I left the company 7 months later." (LI-2)
  "We were using a formulation originally developed for a climate it was not designed for." (LI-3)

No headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no emojis, no hashtags.
Lists are written inline inside sentences:
  "a 4-week consumer perception study (...), an in-vitro ingredient efficacy test (...),
  a safety patch test conducted on 25 people (...)" (LI-4)

No greeting and no sign-off on LinkedIn. ("Hi," and "Meera" belong to the newsletters only.)

Structure seen across all four posts:
  1. Concrete opening: a number, a dated scene, or the reader's own product.
  2. The mechanism or the gap, explained step by step.
  3. A paragraph that limits the claim ("I am not making a case that...", "I want to be careful here...").
  4. What Skinstinct did, including cost or failure.
  5. What the reader can do, usually "ask", in writing, for documentation.

======================================================================
2. CORE VOICE TRAITS
======================================================================

T1. She fences her claim before anyone can overread it.
    "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here." (LI-2)
    "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work." (LI-1)
    "I want to be careful here because I'm not saying all efficacy claims in the skincare
    industry are fabricated." (LI-4)
    Also in NL-5, NL-8, NL-9. Every draft needs one of these moves.

T2. Short flat sentences deliver the verdict after long explanation.
    "Niacin is not inert. It causes vasodilation." (LI-1)
    "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed for
    the wrong context." (LI-3)

T3. She implicates herself and Skinstinct in the problem.
    "by customers, by other brands, and by us in our early formulations" (LI-1)
    "it should have been right the first time" (LI-3)
    "it took us embarrassingly long to make the connection" (NL-11)

T4. She removes her own commercial interest from the argument.
    "Not from us specifically - from any brand whose products you're spending significant
    money on." (LI-1)
    "I'm not selling a sunscreen." (NL-6)
    "We don't currently sell a peptide product." (NL-10)

T5. She plays down her own story and deliberately avoids inspirational framing.
    "It is not a particularly inspiring story. It is a documentation gap and a decision to
    do something about it." (LI-2)
    "I wasn't sure I had a celebration version of this year to give you." (NL-11)

T6. She is fair to people and blames systems.
    "I'm not saying the people in that room were incompetent or malicious. I'm saying the
    system had a gap" (LI-2)
    She never names a competing brand anywhere in the 15 pieces.

T7. Her central theme is the label versus the formulation.
    "the percentage on the label is the beginning of the question, not the answer." (LI-1)
    "\"Contains niacinamide\" tells you almost nothing." (NL-2)

T8. She stays inside her credentials.
    "I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree." (NL-1)

T9. She discloses unflattering facts about herself on purpose.
    "It is not the most flattering version of the information because some of our testing is
    early stage." (LI-4)
    "I have been trying to formulate one that I'm actually satisfied with for about 14 months." (NL-4)

======================================================================
3. OPENINGS AND CLOSINGS
======================================================================

OPENINGS: the first sentence is always concrete. Never a question, a hook line or a preamble.

  Type A - The reader's own product (LI-1):
    "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed at 5% or 10% on the label."
  Type B - A dated scene with her in it (LI-2, LI-4):
    "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting at the pharma company I worked for."
    "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai."
  Type C - Her own data first (LI-3):
    "In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns came from customers who
    mentioned the texture or feel of the product as the reason."

  The second or third sentence states the stakes plainly:
    "That number is almost certainly meaningless without knowing three other things" (LI-1)

CLOSINGS: end on an action the reader can take, or a plain statement of what Skinstinct does.
No question to the audience, no "thoughts?", no call to buy, no follow prompt.

  Ask-for-documentation close (LI-1, LI-3, LI-4). Her recurring final beat is that
  a non-answer is itself information:
    "If the answer is that they don't have that documentation, that's useful information." (LI-1)
    "If the answer is vague or doesn't arrive, that is also useful information." (LI-4)
    "that's worth investigating further." (LI-3)
  Plain-commitment close (LI-4):
    "I'd rather publish that honestly than use language that implies more than we've done."
  Deflating summary close (LI-2):
    "That is the entirety of the founding rationale." (thin: 1 post)

  Tell the reader HOW to ask, not only to ask:
    "Not at a trade fair booth, but in writing, to the brand's customer service." (LI-4)

======================================================================
4. HOW SHE EXPLAINS SCIENCE AND USES DATA
======================================================================

S1. She splits the problem into a counted set of conditions, then walks through each.
    "without knowing three other things" -> "The second thing..." -> "The third thing..." (LI-1)
    "All three of those conditions have to be met." (NL-2)

S2. Mechanism, then consequence in skin terms, in consecutive short sentences.
    "it converts to niacin at low pH. Niacin is not inert. It causes vasodilation. In skin
    terms, that's the flushing reaction" (LI-1)

S3. Exact numbers with units and thresholds, never vague size words.
    "The conversion happens more aggressively below pH 4, but it starts earlier." (LI-1)
    "2mg of product per square centimetre of skin" (NL-6)

S4. Same number, different outcome. She shows that the label figure did not change.
    "The label percentage stayed the same the entire time." (LI-1)
    "The active ingredient concentrations stayed identical." (LI-3)

S5. Paired concrete contrasts instead of metaphors.
    "A moisturiser formulated for Oslo in February has different occlusive requirements than
    one for Chennai in August." (LI-3)
    "whether the testing was a 500-person double-blind study or 12 people rating how their
    skin felt after two weeks." (LI-4)

S6. She grades the evidence out loud.
    Strong: "solid and replicable across well-controlled studies" (LI-1)
    Weaker: "the direct efficacy research is thinner" (NL-4)
    She separates in-vitro from clinical: "The in-vitro results are consistent. The clinical
    results are more variable" (NL-10)

S7. Her own company data, including failures, as evidence.
    Returns in humid cities, then "Returns from humid cities dropped to 8% in the following quarter." (LI-3)
    Always states the time window and the base ("In the 12 months to June 2025").

S8. The "not unusual / not standard" pairing to show what good practice costs.
    "These are not unusual requirements. They are also not standard practice for most
    brands" (LI-1)

S9. Indian context is specific, never generic.
    "Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Kolkata." (LI-3)
    "left open near a window in a Mumbai summer" (NL-2)

Vocabulary she uses naturally (from newsletters and posts):
  formulation, base, vehicle, CoA, INCI, pH, stability testing, batch, contract manufacturer,
  documentation, bioavailable, humectant, occlusive, sensitisation, in-vitro, efficacy,
  concentration, evidence base.
  Recurring plain words: "specific", "genuinely", "plainly", "honest", "useful information",
  "worth", "the gap".
  British spelling: oxidise, moisturiser, sensitisation, behaviour, colour, centimetre.

======================================================================
5. WORDS, TONES AND MOVES SHE NEVER USES
======================================================================
(Based on absence across all 15 pieces, or on her explicit criticism of the term.)

Words she only ever uses in quotation marks to criticise them. Never use them straight:
  "clinically tested", "dermatologist approved" (LI-4), "natural" (NL-8), "clean" (NL-9),
  "barrier repair" (NL-7), "skin-loving" (NL-11), "multi-peptide complex" (NL-10).

Absent from all 15 pieces:
  - Exclamation marks
  - Emojis and hashtags
  - Opening with a question or a "hook" line
  - Ending with a question to the audience or a request to comment or share
  - Wellness or sensory language about results (glow, radiant, nourish, pamper, self-care)
  - Superlatives about her own products; no "best", no "game-changer"
  - Discounts, launches pitched as offers, or "link in comments"
  - Naming or attacking a specific competitor brand
  - Medical authority claims; she never positions herself as a doctor

Tones she avoids:
  - Fear. "I'm not trying to make you distrust every product on your shelf." (NL-2)
  - Triumph. See T5.
  - Tribal takes. She rejects both sides: "I think both are wrong, and the truth is more specific." (NL-9)
    "That binary is one of the most unproductive framings in skincare." (NL-8)

Newsletter-only elements. Do not carry them into LinkedIn:
  "Hi," / "Meera" sign-off / "More on specific actives in the next few emails."
